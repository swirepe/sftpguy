How to listen closely

Notice the restricted frequency range, surface noise, and uneven dynamics. Compare the human voices with the sound of Big Ben. These defects are evidence of the recording apparatus and later transfer, not simply “bad audio.”

About authenticity

Audio files are historical digitizations credited to their source repositories. This interface, labels, artwork, rotation and controls are a contemporary interpretive reconstruction, not a functioning simulation of wax-cylinder mechanics.

A compact history

When sound first became a collectible object

1877

Thomas Edison demonstrates a phonograph capable of recording and reproducing sound, initially using tinfoil.

1880s–1890s

Wax-cylinder machines develop into commercial recording systems. Captured speech, music, novelty recordings and talking toys coexist.

1900s

Phonographs become mass-market domestic machines; advertisements begin to sell the experience of hearing a voice at home.

Field journal & provenance

This time capsule preserves a small but coherent journey through early recorded sound: a clock heard in 1890, a voice made for a talking doll, and a talking-machine advertisement from about 1906. These recordings survive through later transfers, which can alter speed, noise and perceived tone. Their digital copies are primary audio artifacts; this web player is modern.

Original collection records: Big Ben · Edison Talking Doll · Advertising Record. See MANIFEST.json for exact upstream files, rights notes and integrity metadata. For broader historical context see Library of Congress National Jukebox and UCSB Cylinder Audio Archive.

All controls work without a network connection when this HTML file and the three OGG files are in the same directory. For maximum offline compatibility, use a modern Firefox or Chromium browser with Ogg/Vorbis support. If audio fails, check that the files were not renamed.
